AThe night in 60 seconds

You join a mixed team, pick one real problem New Yorkers have, build a small working app that uses free city data, then pitch it to judges.

Nobody expects a finished product. Judges reward three things: a real, relatable problem, a demo that actually works (even a tiny one), and a clear story about who it helps. Two of those three are not coding. That is where you shine.

Sponsor tip: RevenueCat is hosting. If your pitch has one slide on "how this app makes money" (a free tier and a paid tier), you are speaking the sponsor's language.

WThe bar to beat: Daytona HackSprint winners

Daytona's HackSprints are one-day AI build sprints (about 5½ hours of hacking, teams of up to 4, 3-minute pitch with a live demo). The latest NYC one was Oct 16, 2026, with 65 projects submitted.

1st place, NYC Oct 16 2026: Just Standup

A fitness app for coders who never make it to the gym. It's built around three excuses, each with a fix. "No device": scan a QR code, hold your phone and punch, and each punch splashes paint on a virtual Brooklyn wall. "No place": an AI agent finds real workout spots around NYC. "No coach": film your room and the AI turns your chair, desk and wall into a safe workout.

Why it won: a personal story everyone in the room recognized, a demo the audience could physically join (no app to install, just a QR code), a New York tie-in, and every sponsor's tool used for a real reason. This was a two-person team, and one of them researches human motion for a living, so the tech was well past one evening's work.

What that means for tonight

Tonight is shorter (evening only). Copy Just Standup's shape, not its size:

  • Open with a story the judges recognize. Just Standup opened with friends' gym excuses. Yours might be "my building had no heat for 6 days."
  • Let the audience touch it. Put a QR code on your last slide so judges open your live app on their own phones.
  • One user, one sentence. "A Bronx renter checks their building's heat history before signing a lease."
  • One live loop: type an address → real NYC data appears → a plain-English answer. That's the whole demo.
  • One "smart" touch: an AI line that summarizes the data ("This building had 14 heat complaints last winter, worse than 80% of the block"). This is your 10xhr moment.
  • One money slide plus a mock Pro screen for RevenueCat.
  • Online at a real link (Vercel) so judges can tap it.

Skip: logins, user accounts, multiple pages, real payments, a mobile app store build. None of the winners above had those, and each one costs an hour you don't have.

BRevenueCat in plain English

RevenueCat is the cash register for apps. When an app says "Upgrade to Pro for $4.99/month," RevenueCat is very often the thing running that behind the scenes.

The problem it solves

Selling a subscription inside an app is painful. Apple, Google, and the web (Stripe) each have their own rules and their own payment code. RevenueCat wraps all three into one system, so a developer writes the "is this person a paying customer?" check once, and it works on iPhone, Android, and web.

What it gives an app

  • Paywalls: the "Go Pro" screen. Built in a drag-and-drop editor, and it now has an AI editor you can chat with to design one.
  • Subscriptions tracking: who paid, who cancelled, who is on a free trial.
  • Web checkout: a no-code payment link you can share, with Apple Pay / Google Pay buttons via Stripe.
  • Funnels: multi-step onboarding web pages ending in checkout (launched June 2026).
  • Charts & experiments: revenue dashboards, and A/B testing different prices.
  • AI toolkit: plugins that let tools like Claude Code set up RevenueCat for you.

Business model and size

Free until an app makes $2,500/month, then roughly 1% of tracked revenue. Founded 2018 in San Francisco, used by tens of thousands of apps. Competitors you may hear: Adapty, Superwall.

Realistic for one night: wiring real App Store payments takes days of approval. Your team does not need that. A mock "Pro" screen in your app, or a RevenueCat web paywall link, plus a slide explaining the paid tier, is plenty.

Smart questions to ask the RevenueCat people

  • "What's the fastest way to add a paywall to a web app tonight?"
  • "What do the best-converting subscription apps have in common?"
  • "Do you see civic or local apps succeed with subscriptions, or is a different model better?"
  • "What roles do you hire for besides engineers?" (networking)

CJargon decoder

Words you will hear in the first 20 minutes, in plain terms.

API
A menu a website offers to other programs. You "order" data with a web address and get data back.
Endpoint
One specific item on that menu, a URL that returns one kind of data.
Open data
Data the city publishes free for anyone: complaints, inspections, trees, crashes.
JSON
The text format APIs reply in. Looks like a labeled list: {"borough":"BRONX"}.
API key
A password-like code some APIs require so they know who is asking. Never paste one in a public place.
Frontend / backend
Frontend is what users see and tap. Backend is the hidden part that fetches and stores data.
MVP
Minimum viable product: the smallest version that proves the idea works.
Deploy
Put the app online at a real link so judges can open it. Vercel is a common one-click option.
Repo / GitHub
The shared folder where the team's code lives, with history of every change.
SDK
A ready-made code kit from a company (RevenueCat has one) so devs don't start from scratch.
Paywall
The screen that asks you to pay to unlock features.
Entitlement
RevenueCat's word for "what this user has unlocked," like Pro.
Freemium
Free basic version, paid upgrade for extras.
Scope creep
Adding features until nothing is finished. The #1 hackathon killer.

FYour role as the non-technical builder

Mixed teams need someone who keeps the project focused and tells its story. Claim that out loud in the first 10 minutes: "I'll own the problem, the user, and the pitch, and I'll test everything as we go."

  • Product lead: write the one-sentence goal on paper and guard it from scope creep.
  • User voice: you are a New Yorker. Your real stories about rats or no heat make the pitch land.
  • Prompter: with Claude Code, describing what you want clearly is building. Write the feature requests in plain English.
  • Tester: click everything, find what breaks, report it before the judges do.
  • Timekeeper: call the "stop building" moment.
  • Pitch and slides: start slides at the halfway mark while devs code.

GPitch template (2–3 minutes)

  1. Hook (15s): a real moment. "Last winter my building had no heat for 6 days."
  2. Problem (20s): who suffers and how many. Pull one number from the data, e.g. how many heat complaints NYC got last year.
  3. Solution (15s): one sentence on what you built.
  4. Live demo (60s): one path, rehearsed. Don't wander.
  5. Data (15s): "Built on NYC Open Data 311, updated daily."
  6. Business model (20s): free tier vs. Pro tier, and the price. This is your RevenueCat moment.
  7. Next steps (15s): what you'd build with another week.

If the demo breaks, say "here's what it looked like 10 minutes ago" and show the backup screenshot. Judges have seen it a hundred times; calm recovery reads as confidence.
